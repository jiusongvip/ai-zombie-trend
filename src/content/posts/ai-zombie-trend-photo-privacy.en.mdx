The trend asks for two faces, and usually one of them belongs to someone else. That is worth a pause rather than a panic. The effect itself is harmless; the real question is the ordinary one — what happens to a picture of a person you handed to a server. Four checks make it a reasonable trade.

## Ask the person in the photo

This is the whole ethics of the format, and it is easier than it sounds.

- **Your own face** needs no permission from anyone.
- **A partner is a co-star.** Ask before you render, not after the clip is in your camera roll, because they are the one who will get tagged in it.
- **Friends are fine with a yes.** A reply that says go ahead is consent; silence is not, and neither is a "whatever" muttered over a phone.
- **Children stay out of it.** A child's face in a film you publish is a decision they cannot review later, and this is the one case where "everyone does it" is no defence.
- **Strangers, ex-partners, colleagues, and anyone cropped in from the background** are not yours to transform.

Skip anything built to pass as real. A clip of a public figure, or one framed as though something happened to a named person, is a different category of problem from a couple doing a bit. The joke only works among people who are in on it.

## Read the retention policy for three answers

You do not need to understand the document, only find three things in it:

1. **How long uploads are kept.** Look for a stated window, or an explicit "kept until you ask us to remove them," plus a way to make that request. "As long as necessary" is not an answer.
2. **Whether your photos train models.** Search the page for the word *train*. A policy worth reading says no in plain terms; a policy that never mentions it is promising nothing.
3. **Who else touches the file.** Rendering means sending your two photos to a video model provider. A good policy names that step and bounds it to the one request, instead of pretending the picture never leaves.

If a tool offers no readable policy where you upload, the silence is the answer. Move on. Our own [privacy policy](/privacy-policy) is written to be read in one pass — the least any tool should offer.

## Why a hash-named object store matters

Uploads keep a name. Yours probably arrived with a camera-roll filename, and in some tools the stored object sits under your account id or email. If that bucket leaks, the file listing alone tells a stranger whose face is whose — no cracking required, just reading.

Content-addressed storage closes that gap. When the stored name comes from a hash of the image itself, it reveals nothing about the person, the uploader, or the original file, and there is no username list to walk. On [AI Zombie Video](/) uploads are stored exactly that way: the filename derives from the image content, not your account or your original name. The same flow lets you stage both photos and choose a story before creating an account, with files up to 10 MB each, so a trial upload asks for no identity — only rendering does.

Two habits finish the job on your side. Check what the frame reveals before you upload — house numbers, uniforms, school signage, a street through a window — and keep the pair you could explain if it were screenshotted.

## Label it, then post it

Wherever a feed offers a label for generated video, switch it on, and say plainly in the caption that the film came from two photos. That is not defensive housekeeping — it is the line that keeps the clip a bit rather than a fake, and it answers the first question every viewer asks, which is how you made it. Point them at the [examples wall](/examples) so the answer is one click away.

A finished film is also just a file on your device: nothing has to be public for you to have made it, and you decide what to keep.

## The pre-render checklist

- Everyone in both photos agreed, in writing, recently.
- Nobody in frame is a child or an unaware bystander.
- The frame itself gives away no address, uniform, or signage.
- The tool states how long it keeps images, an explicit no on training, and who receives them.
- Uploads are stored under content-derived names rather than account names.
- The AI label is on and the caption says the video was generated.

If all six hold, the trend is a two-photo game and nothing more.
