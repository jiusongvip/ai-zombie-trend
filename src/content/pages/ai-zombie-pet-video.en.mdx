## The four shots, in order

The pet story keeps the same skeleton as the couple, friends and family versions —
same locations, same seconds, same match cut. Only the animal-specific lines change.

| Shot                | Seconds | What happens                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — The cabin       | 0–4     | The owner stands trembling, gun lowered at their side, unable to raise it. Cut wide: the pet drags itself across the room closer — closer, not attacking.                           |
| 2 — The undead face | 4–6     | Extreme close-up. Pale grey fur, cloudy white eyes, dark veins, disheveled coat. Frightening, and still unmistakably your animal.                                                   |
| 3 — The choice      | 6–10    | The owner lets the gun fall and opens both arms. The pet stops; something familiar flickers in those cloudy eyes. It comes the last few steps and rests its head on their shoulder. |
| 4 — The match cut   | 10–15   | Mid-embrace, hard cut: the cabin becomes a golden wheat field at sunset. Both are alive and healthy, the pet’s breed, coat and size restored exactly as in the reference.           |

Cold blue-grey light for the first ten seconds, warm gold for the last five. The colour
change does most of the emotional work; the performance only has to stay still enough
for the cut to read as one hug.

## What actually holds a dog or cat’s identity

Four shots, two lighting setups and a hard cut is a lot of chances for an animal’s face
to drift. The identity sentence has to name the things a model can latch onto:

- **Breed, coat, size** — stated once, early, and repeated inside the final shot, which
  is where drift shows up.
- **Facial identity** — muzzle shape, ear set, eye placement. “The same dog” is not an
  instruction; “the exact breed, coat, size and facial identity of the pet” is.
- **The undead look is painted on top, not substituted.** Grey fur and cloudy eyes are
  layered onto _that_ animal, so shot 2 is still recognisable.
- **The owner is locked too.** Their face, hairstyle and body proportions hold against
  the first reference. A film where the dog is right and the human is wrong still looks
  broken.

## The two photographs that work

Everything downstream depends on these two files.

**Reference one — you.** Shoulders-up, face toward the camera, daylight or an even room
light. No heavy filter, no sunglasses, no hat. The model has to re-render this face in
four shots, so a clear front view does more than a flattering one.

**Reference two — the dog or cat.** A full-body photo taken at the animal's eye level
works best, because one frame has to carry both halves of the identity: the face (muzzle
shape, ear set, eye placement) and the body (breed, coat, size). One animal in the frame,
and nothing covering the face — a bandana, a costume, a toy held in the mouth, or a paw
over the nose all remove features the model has to keep. Standing over the pet and
shooting down loses both the face and the proportions.

Two things help more than people expect: similar brightness in both photos, and a
recent photo of the pet rather than a blurry one from years ago.

## Where pet renders fail, and the line that fixes each one

| What you get                                          | The fix                                                                                                    |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A different breed in the wheat field                  | Restate breed, coat and size **inside the last shot**, not only at the top                                 |
| The pet rises onto two legs or shuffles like a person | Add “moves on all fours” to the approach line                                                              |
| The pet lunges, or the scene reads as an attack       | Keep both guards: “but not attacking” on the approach, “does not attack” before the embrace                |
| The owner’s face drifts                               | The owner needs their own lock — face, hairstyle, body proportions against the first reference             |
| A second animal wanders into frame                    | Put exactly one animal in the reference photo and state “no other animals”                                 |
| The ending looks like two unrelated clips             | Put the continuity instruction on the embrace itself: same arms, same distance, same direction of movement |

## Why the undead pet comes closer instead of attacking

Two reasons, and the second is practical.

The story is grief and recognition, not threat. An animal that charges turns the third
shot into a chase, and then opening both arms means self-defence rather than a choice.
The approach is what the hug is answering.

The other reason is that a film with no gore, no biting and no blood is what most video
models and payment networks will actually process. The restraint is not timidity — it
is what keeps the format publishable, and it is why the trend reads as a love story
dressed as a horror clip rather than the reverse.

## Questions people ask about AI zombie pet videos

### Can the pet be a cat?

Yes. The pet story is written for a dog **or** a cat matching the second reference —
the identity line names breed, coat, size and facial identity, which are the same four
things whether the animal has ears forward or a shorter muzzle. Use a photo where the
face is as clear as the body.

### Can I use a photo of a pet that has died?

A single photograph is all the render needs, and plenty of people make this film about a
pet they lost. Nothing about the output claims the animal is real again — it is fifteen
seconds of light in a wheat field. If you are including yourself in the first reference,
that is your own likeness to consent for.

### Can both references be pets?

No. The survivor in the first shot is always a person — the story is built on someone
lowering the gun. Two animals produce a film with nobody to make that choice.

### How long is the video, and what shape?

Fifteen seconds, four shots, vertical 9:16 — the shape Reels, Shorts and TikTok want —
with ambience and score rather than a silent clip.
