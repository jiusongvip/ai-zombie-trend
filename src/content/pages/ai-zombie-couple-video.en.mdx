## The four shots, in order

The couple story uses the same skeleton as every other film on this site — same four
beats, same seconds, same match cut — and the world you pick (cabin, grey shore, rainy
street, snowy doorstep) is shared across subjects. What belongs to the couple version is
who is on screen and how the contact reads.

| Shot                | Seconds | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------- | ------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 — The two of them | 0–4     | The survivor stands trembling, the weapon or the held object lowered at their side, unable to use it. Cut wide: their partner waits, motionless, facing them — not attacking.                             |
| 2 — The undead face | 4–6     | Extreme close-up on the partner. Pale grey skin, cloudy white eyes, dark veins, disheveled hair. Frightening, and still unmistakably the person in your second photo.                                     |
| 3 — The choice      | 6–10    | The survivor lets go of what they were holding and opens both arms wide. Something familiar flickers in those cloudy eyes. The partner runs the last few metres and wraps their arms tightly around them. |
| 4 — The match cut   | 10–15   | At the precise moment of the embrace, hard cut: the cabin becomes a sunlit wheat field. Both are alive and healthy, both faces exactly as in the references, in clean everyday clothing.                  |

Cold blue-grey light for the first ten seconds, warm gold for the last five. The colour
change carries most of the emotion; the two performances only have to stay still enough
for the cut to read as one embrace rather than two clips.

## How both faces stay yours

The identity instruction in the couple template names four things, for **both**
characters, and repeats the lock at the end where drift shows up:

- **Facial identity** — each face against its own reference, not a blend of the two.
- **Hairstyle and body proportions** — the two things that make two faces of similar age
  read as two different people.
- **Who is who** — the first photo is the survivor, the second is the partner. The model
  is told the survivor cannot raise the weapon and the partner never moves behind them,
  so the two roles stay on opposite sides of the frame.
- **The undead look is painted on top, not substituted** — grey skin and cloudy eyes go
  onto _that_ person, which is why shot 2 is still recognisable as your partner.

Nothing in the story assumes a gender or an orientation. The two references decide who
these people are, so a straight couple, a same-sex couple, or two people whose
relationship you would rather not label all run through the same template.

## The two photographs that work

**Reference one — the survivor.** Shoulders-up, face toward the camera, daylight or an
even room light. No heavy filter, no sunglasses, no hat brim across the eyes. This face
gets re-rendered in four shots, so a clear front view does more work than a flattering
one.

**Reference two — the partner.** The same rules, because this face carries even more: it
has to survive the undead makeup in shot 2 and the hard cut in shot 4. A photo where the
eyes, jawline and hairline are all visible is worth more than a candid where the face is
turned away.

Two things that matter more for a couple than for any other subject: **brightness and
colour grade should be similar in both photos** (one sunlit beach shot and one dim
indoor shot push the model toward inventing a third lighting setup), and **avoid a photo
of the two of you together as either reference** — a frame with both faces in it gives
the model no way to tell which face belongs to which role.

## Where couple renders fail, and the line that fixes each one

| What you get                                              | The fix                                                                                                                         |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The two faces converge into one mid-clip look             | Lock each face to its own reference by name (Character A, Character B) instead of “the couple”                                  |
| The partner’s hair or jawline is taken from the survivor  | Name hairstyle and body proportions in the identity line, and again inside the final shot                                       |
| The survivor’s face drifts while the partner holds        | The survivor needs their own lock — face, hairstyle, body proportions against the first reference                               |
| One of them ends up behind or beside the other at the hug | Keep the facing clause: the partner always faces the survivor and never moves behind them                                       |
| The ending looks like two unrelated clips                 | Put continuity on the embrace itself: same arms, same distance, same direction of movement across the cut                       |
| The approach reads as an attack instead of a choice       | Keep both guards: “they do not attack” before the run, and the all-scenes clause that the only contact is running into the arms |

## Why the zombified partner runs closer instead of attacking

Because the hug is what the film is about. An approach that charges turns the third shot
into a struggle, and then opening both arms stops being a choice and becomes
self-defence. The whole beat map is built on the survivor putting the weapon down first,
and on something familiar flickering in those cloudy eyes before the run.

There is a second, practical reason: no excessive gore, no graphic wounds, no biting, no
blood anywhere in the four shots. That restraint is what makes a couple film something
you can post on the short-video platforms it is made for, and why the trend reads as a
love story dressed as a horror clip rather than the reverse.

Yes. The generator’s subject switch is preselected on **a person**, and the second photo

### Can both photos be of my partner and me?

That is exactly the intended use: one photo of you as the survivor, one of your partner
as the one who has turned. Both of you appear in the same film, and consent for your own
likeness is yours to give.

### Can I make it about a partner who has died?

A single photograph is all the render needs, and this is one of the films people make
about someone they lost. Nothing in the output claims the person is real again — it is
fifteen seconds of light in a wheat field. If the second photo is somebody else’s face
and they are still living, that is their consent to give.

### Does it work for a same-sex couple?

Yes. The template refers to both characters with they/them and states that their genders,
appearances and relationship are whatever the supplied references show, so nothing has to
be configured.

lowers and the light the match cut resolves into, not the story.
